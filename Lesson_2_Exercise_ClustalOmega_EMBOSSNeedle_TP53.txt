BACKGROUND
--------------------------------------------------------------
You want to identify which regions of TP53 are conserved
across vertebrate evolution. Conserved positions are likely
functionally essential — mutating them is lethal or causes
disease. This is exactly the analysis researchers use to
identify critical functional domains and hotspot mutation
sites in cancer.

TOOL
--------------------------------------------------------------
URL: www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/clustalo

QUESTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. Look at the alignment output.
    The asterisk (*) marks perfectly conserved positions —
    same amino acid in ALL five species.
    Which region has the most asterisks?
    N-terminal transactivation domain, DNA-binding domain,
    tetramerization domain, or C-terminal regulatory domain?

Q2. Download or view the phylogenetic tree
    (Results → Phylogenetic Tree tab).
    Which two species cluster closest together?
    Does this match what you know about evolution?

Q3. Find a column with an asterisk (*) in the DNA-binding
    domain (approximately residues 102–292 of the human
    sequence). What amino acid is it?
    Why would this position be conserved across
    400 million years of vertebrate evolution?

Q4. Now find a column with no conservation symbol —
    a column where all five species have different
    amino acids. In which part of the protein is it located?
    What does lack of conservation tell you about
    the functional importance of that position?

Q5. The N-terminal transactivation domain is the least
    conserved region across species.
    What does this suggest about the evolution of
    TP53's regulatory interactions?

Q6. Compare the DNA-binding domain across all five species.
    Is this domain more conserved than the
    N-terminal domain?
    Explain why in terms of natural selection.

Q7. Many cancer mutations in TP53 cluster in the
    DNA-binding domain — at positions R175, G245, R248,
    R249, R273, R282.
    Look at these positions in your alignment.
    Are they conserved across all five species?
    What does this tell you about why mutations
    here are so dangerous?

EXPECTED RESULTS (read after answering)
--------------------------------------------------------------
Most conserved:  DNA-binding domain — direct contact
                 with DNA response elements
Least conserved: N-terminal transactivation domain
                 and C-terminal regulatory domain
Tree:            human and chicken cluster together,
                 then mouse, then frog and zebrafish
Hotspot positions: R175, R248, R273 are perfectly
                 conserved across all five species —
                 confirming their essential function
Conclusion:      evolutionary constraint keeps the
                 DNA-binding domain conserved; mutations
                 here disrupt a function critical to
                 all vertebrate life


==============================================================
  CONNECTING THE EXERCISES
==============================================================

After completing both exercises, answer these final questions:

FINAL Q1.
BLASTp identified the protein from its sequence alone.
Clustal Omega showed you conservation across five species.
Which tool gives you more information about which positions
are functionally essential? Why?

FINAL Q2.
You found that the DNA-binding domain is the most conserved
region in both exercises. This is also where most cancer
mutations occur. Is this a coincidence?
What is the relationship between evolutionary conservation,
mutation hotspots, and drug target identification?

FINAL Q3.
Several drugs targeting mutant TP53 are currently in
clinical trials — including APR-246 (eprenetapopt),
which tries to restore the folding of mutant TP53.
Based on your alignment results — why does it make sense
to target the DNA-binding domain specifically?
What problem might arise if the drug also affects
conserved residues shared with other p53 family members
(p63, p73)?
